How to Teach Your Child Telugu When You Live Abroad

Seven habits that help children abroad keep their Telugu.

21 October 2026 · 5 min read

Many Telugu parents in the USA, UK and elsewhere share the same worry: the children understand a little Telugu, but they answer in English. By the time they are ten, the language is slipping away. The good news is that a few steady habits make a real difference. Here is what works.

1. Make one place or time “Telugu only”

Children learn a language when they need it. Pick a small, fixed space where Telugu is the only language: dinner, the car ride to school, or video calls with grandparents. Keep it short and consistent rather than ambitious. Fifteen minutes every day beats an hour once a week.

2. Talk to grandparents often, and give the child a job

Calls with grandparents are the strongest motivation most children have. Before a call, give your child one thing to say in Telugu: what they ate, a game they played, a word they learned. Small successes build confidence.

3. Teach the script early, but keep it playful

Speaking comes first, but reading opens the door to stories and songs. Start with the Telugu alphabet (Varnamala) a few letters at a time. Write letters with chalk, find them in signs and labels, and turn practice into a game rather than homework.

4. Use songs, rhymes and stories

Children remember rhythm far better than lists. Traditional rhymes, festival songs and bedtime stories read aloud in Telugu build vocabulary without feeling like study. Repeat favourites; repetition is how language sticks.

5. Connect the language to festivals and food

Sankranti, Ugadi and family celebrations are natural moments to use Telugu words for food, clothes and customs. When a language is tied to things a child enjoys, it stops feeling like a school subject.

6. Don’t correct every mistake

If a child gets laughed at or corrected constantly, they switch to English. Praise the effort, repeat the sentence back correctly without comment, and move on.

7. Get a teacher when home practice isn’t enough

Busy parents can’t always teach grammar, reading and writing step by step. A live class gives your child structure, a teacher who corrects pronunciation in the moment, and other children to speak with. Small groups work best, because every child gets time to speak.
